  18. ^report(14) · 2026-10-14
    What the page keeps from one edit to the next

    A tab on #2597 keeps about 92 MB more after every slider edit on Full Body, without levelling off. It is not the caches (each tried in a build of its own) and not any long-lived table (each counted after every edit): what stays is whole versions of the editor's state, made by every stage of an edit. Which reference holds them is still open, since a heap snapshot kills this tab; the lead is ValueHash's WeakMap of about two million entries. Updated later on 2026-10-14: a heap snapshot named it, the undo history, which kept a whole page model per edit, uncapped by default. Andrew Blinn's compaction, ported, with the newest undo entry kept whole so undo stays as fast as before, keeps the tab level: 208 MB after 12 edits, against 1,048. Both are on #2597 and #2635.

  21. ^report(11) · 2026-10-13
    Naming what a slider leaves alone

    Every slider on three Livelit Playground slides, 63 edits: what Hazel's own reuse saves (on Full Body and Witch, little: the figure is one binding), what a memo keyed by names could save on top, and how that depends on the names. After Fumola's merge sort, four ways to name a call: by position, by caller, by operand and by content. They agree on edits that only change values; on edits that change which calls are made, naming a call for its operand comes closest to content. And the picture's own representation sets its diff: with groups and transforms instead of a flat list of shapes, every slider edit would change a handful of nodes. Preliminary: steps, with every check free.

  23. ^report(9) · 2026-10-13
    Names and reuse: what fresh ids cost Hazel

    How do fresh ids relate to lack of reuse? The question of Incremental Computation with Names (OOPSLA 2015), asked of Hazel. The editor names the program's syntax; statics and the evaluator mint gensyms for the rest. Analyzing a slide again renames hundreds of nodes, and naming two sites in statics cuts that by seven eighths. But the reuse check falls back to comparing structure, so the same parts are reused either way: in Hazel today, fresh ids cost time, in the check, in the 461,422 ids one woods evaluation mints, and in what crosses to the worker, not reuse.

  24. ^report(8) · 2026-10-12
    One slider, three ways: what modular editing saves

    One slider change on the Livelit Playground's Full Body slide, three ways. On #2597, without modular editing, it takes 8.6 s, mostly statics on the whole program. On #2635, with it, 1.2 s. Showing only the slider and the picture should be faster still, but it took 3.5 s: opening them as cells gives the picture new ids on every change, and the worker's reuse check then compared lexemes in a walk that was quadratic over the program's lets. Comparing them in the same memoized walk as structure, now on #2635, brings it to 1.26 s, as fast as the whole program. Updated later on 2026-10-14: measured again with the same fixes in both, showing only the GUI was slower again, by the autosave, whose print of a slide with probes had become quadratic in its top-level items; with that fixed (about 1 s to 80 ms a change), the two take the same time, as ^report(15) found again on a newer head on 2026-10-15.

  25. ^report(7) · 2026-10-12
    Five minutes to the first picture

    On #2597, the full livelits branch, the Livelit Playground's Scenes slide took about five minutes to draw its first picture. It really does, 287.5 s against 12.5 s on #2635, and the reason is that the page redraws everything for every message from the worker. #2597's worker sends one after every slice of its evaluation: 2,520 while the slide loads, each costing the page about 140 ms, mostly rebuilding the code editor's view of the whole program. The worker sits idle while the page catches up. One commit from #2469, which sends at most one message every 100 ms, brings the first picture to about 17 s on a copy of #2597.

  26. ^report(6) · 2026-10-12
    Catching performance regressions in CI

    ^report(1)–^report(5) found and fixed performance problems by hand. Nothing in Hazel's CI stops them coming back: no check is required to merge, and the two benchmark workflows never worked on dev. A time can't fail a build on GitHub's runners, where the same tests vary by about 10% from run to run; a count of the work done can. The first such check counts the evaluator's steps and holds them to budgets, and it fails when reuse breaks. Also: building on an earlier benchmarking PR, or starting fresh, compared in a table.

  27. ^report(5) · 2026-10-12
    Two threads, one picture: the worker, the page, and diffing the view

    Hazel evaluates a program in a web worker and draws it on the page's main thread. Each fix so far, sorted by thread, and compared with a more principled idea: the worker diffs each view and sends the page only patches. A worker cannot build real DOM, only data, and Hazel's Html value is already that data. What crosses between the threads already carries only what changed. What is still done whole on every change is the page's: evaluating each changed view, converting it to virtual DOM, and diffing it. Updated the same day: measured in the woods, evaluating the view is about 69% of the page's time, and converting and diffing about 2%. So the next step is a cheaper view.

    The witch and her woods, ported to Hazel

    M's witch is now a Livelit Playground slide, with her colors, her nose, her cat and her broom, and her woods and three more of the game's backgrounds are a Scenes slide. A witch redraw takes 5–7.5 s. One in the woods took 10–21 s, even when only the figure moved, against 1–3 ms in the game. Three causes were found and fixed.
